A: Your requirements are already a good foundation. The main improvement I'd make is to define the task more precisely: the skill should produce a faithful, edited article, not a summary, a rewrite, or an independently researched article.

This distinction matters because LLMs tend to compress information, eliminate repetition too aggressively, smooth over contradictions, and occasionally replace the speaker's reasoning with their own.

I reviewed your requirements against the attached Obsidian Web Clipper transcript. There are several ambiguities worth resolving before implementing the skill.

## 1. Issues and proposed improvements

| Area                 | Issue or risk                                                                             | Recommendation                                                                                                        |

| Transcription errors | The model may confidently "correct" an unfamiliar name or technical term incorrectly.     | Correct only when reasonably confident. Preserve uncertain passages and flag consequential ambiguities.               |
| Readability          | Aggressive rewriting can change meaning, emphasis, and the speaker's personality.         | Define two modes: Conservative and Polished, with identical content-preservation requirements.                        |
| Perspective          | Preserving first/third person is not sufficient to preserve the speaker's viewpoint.      | Also preserve voice, degree of certainty, opinions, rhetorical intent, and attribution.                               |
| Redundancy           | Some repetition is useless; other repetition develops an argument.                        | Remove redundant wording but retain repetitions that add information, clarification, or emphasis.                     |
| Structure            | Original order and thematic organization can sometimes conflict.                          | Preserve the original sequence of ideas. Group adjacent material only; do not move arguments between sections.        |

### Important observations from your example

Your sample reveals three particularly relevant problems.

1\. The transcript language differs from the video's apparent original language.

The metadata and description are Russian, while the transcript is English. This means "preserve the language of the transcript" would produce an English article, even though the original video may have been recorded in Russian.

I recommend making the transcript's language authoritative by default, rather than trying to infer the video's original language. This is predictable and avoids accidental translation.

2\. Metadata and transcript contain different types of information.

The video description contains promotional content, contact details, and a list of topics, while the transcript contains the actual discussion.

The description should not be merged into the article or used to fill gaps in the transcript. It can, however, provide useful context for correcting terminology and names.

3\. The transcript contains potentially questionable regulatory claims.

For example, it describes a 2030 cross-border invoicing deadline and suggests that sent invoices cannot be corrected. Such claims deserve careful treatment because they concern legal requirements and can be oversimplified.

The conversion should preserve what the speaker said, while the critique can flag claims that require verification. It should not automatically substitute a different legal interpretation.

## 2. Additional decisions I recommend

A few choices are especially important for a reliable Codex skill.

Two readability modes, not two different summarization levels. Both should preserve the same information. The conservative mode performs minimal linguistic editing, while the polished mode restructures sentences and paragraphs more extensively. This makes comparison meaningful.

No arbitrary compression target. Avoid requirements such as "reduce the transcript by 40%." The appropriate reduction depends on how much filler the original contains. A dense technical lecture might barely shrink; a conversational interview could shrink substantially.

No invented explanations. The skill should assume a knowledgeable reader, but that does not mean it should remove explanations the speaker actually provided. It should preserve them without adding elementary background material.

Source traceability. Preserve useful timestamps at section boundaries or for significant claims. This is particularly valuable when the critique identifies something questionable and you want to locate the original passage.

Critique should be optional and evidence-aware. Internal contradictions can be detected without external tools. Verification of laws, dates, scientific claims, and other external facts is a separate activity. A useful critique must distinguish these cases rather than treating every model suspicion as a confirmed error.

## 3. Revised requirements

Below is the corrected version, written as a standalone specification suitable for later conversion into a Codex skill.

# YouTube Transcript to Article — Requirements

## 1. Purpose

Convert YouTube transcripts saved as Markdown files by Obsidian Web Clipper into coherent, readable, well-structured articles.

The goal is to **preserve the original informational content and the speaker's reasoning while removing artifacts of spoken language and transcription**.

The result should be a faithful edited version of the original presentation, not a summary, an independent interpretation, or a newly researched article.

The target reader is assumed to have sufficient background knowledge of the subject.

## 2. Input Processing

The input is a Markdown file created by Obsidian Web Clipper.

It may contain:

- YAML frontmatter with metadata such as title, source URL, author, publication date, description, and tags.
- Video embeds, descriptions, promotional text, and other material preceding the transcript.
- A `## Transcript` section containing timestamped transcript entries.
- Optional headings and subsections within the transcript.

Requirements:

1. Identify the transcript section and process it as the primary source of article content.
2. Preserve useful source metadata, including title, video URL, author/channel, and publication date when available.
3. Use metadata and descriptions as contextual information for interpreting names, terminology, and ambiguous passages, but do not automatically incorporate their claims into the article.
4. Exclude promotional material outside the transcript unless explicitly requested.
5. Preserve the original file without modification.

## 3. Content Fidelity

**Preservation of meaning and substantive information takes priority over brevity and stylistic improvements.**

Retain:

- Facts, figures, dates, names, and technical details.
- Claims, hypotheses, assumptions, and conclusions.
- Key insights and observations.
- Opinions, viewpoints, and evaluations.
- Arguments, supporting evidence, and counterarguments.
- Intermediate reasoning, causal relationships, and chains of thought expressed by the speaker.
- Examples, analogies, comparisons, and illustrative scenarios.
- Procedures, processes, instructions, and sequences of steps.
- Qualifications, exceptions, limitations, and expressions of uncertainty.

Do not:

- Summarize or condense substantive material merely to make the article shorter.
- Remove examples or reasoning because their conclusions have already been stated.
- Replace the speaker's reasoning with a more elegant or convincing argument.
- Introduce new facts, explanations, arguments, or conclusions.
- Resolve contradictions by silently selecting one of the conflicting statements.
- Strengthen or weaken the certainty of claims.
- Change the speaker's position or intended meaning.

When the speaker's reasoning is incomplete or unclear, preserve what is actually expressed rather than inventing missing logical steps.

## 4. Transcription Correction

Correct obvious transcription errors when the intended meaning can be established with reasonable confidence.

This includes:

- Grammar and punctuation errors.
- Incorrect sentence boundaries.
- Misrecognized words and phrases.
- Incorrectly transcribed technical terminology.
- Proper names, abbreviations, and acronyms when sufficiently identifiable.
- Numbers and units when the intended values are clear from context.

Use contextual information from the transcript and metadata to resolve ambiguities.

Do not guess when multiple interpretations are plausible.

For uncertain passages:

- Preserve the most defensible interpretation without introducing unsupported specificity.
- Flag consequential ambiguities in a separate editorial note or critique.
- Avoid burdening the article with notes about minor, inconsequential uncertainties.

Never silently change a substantive claim merely because it appears factually incorrect.

## 5. Removal of Irrelevant Material

Remove material that does not contribute to the substantive content, including:

- Filler words and speech disfluencies.
- False starts and abandoned sentence fragments that add no meaning.
- Unnecessary greetings and farewells.
- Requests to like, subscribe, comment, or share.
- Advertisements and promotional remarks unrelated to the subject.
- References to the recording process that add no useful information.
- Repeated phrases that add no information, clarification, or meaningful emphasis.
- Conversational transitions without informational value.

Preserve:

- Repetitions that introduce additional nuance or clarification.
- Rhetorical questions that help develop an argument.
- Meaningful emphasis.
- Relevant personal experiences and anecdotes.
- References to earlier or later parts of the discussion when needed for comprehension.

Avoid over-filtering conversational material that contributes to the author's reasoning or presentation.

## 6. Readability and Editing Modes

Support two editing modes for experimentation.

### Mode A — Conservative

Prioritize fidelity to the speaker's original wording and style.

- Correct transcription, grammar, and punctuation errors.
- Remove irrelevant phrases and redundant wording.
- Reconstruct broken sentences.
- Divide long passages into readable paragraphs.
- Preserve original sentence structures where practical.
- Make only minimal stylistic changes.

### Mode B — Polished

Prioritize readability and coherence while preserving the same substantive content.

In addition to conservative editing:

- Rewrite awkward sentences into natural written language.
- Improve sentence structure and paragraph organization.
- Clarify references and relationships already established in the transcript.
- Combine fragmented statements into coherent explanations.
- Improve transitions between related ideas.
- Reduce unnecessary linguistic complexity.

Both modes must preserve the same substantive information, reasoning, examples, and conclusions.

Neither mode should introduce explanations, remove meaningful details, or change the author's intended meaning.

The editing mode must be configurable, with Conservative as the default.

## 7. Language, Perspective, and Voice

1. Preserve the language of the transcript. Do not translate automatically.
2. Preserve the speaker's grammatical perspective, including first-person and third-person narration.
3. Preserve the speaker's opinions, attitudes, and degree of certainty.
4. Preserve attribution when the speaker reports someone else's claims or opinions.
5. Maintain the speaker's characteristic tone where it contributes to meaning, while removing unnecessary artifacts of spontaneous speech.
6. Do not convert subjective statements into objective facts.
7. Do not introduce an external narrator unless the original material requires one.

The result should read as an edited version of the speaker's own presentation rather than an article written by someone else about the presentation.

## 8. Structure and Organization

Organize the article into meaningful sections when the content supports such division.

Requirements:

- Preserve the original sequence of topics, arguments, and examples.
- Do not reorder content to create a more conventional article structure.
- Group adjacent, closely related material into paragraphs or sections.
- Use descriptive headings that accurately reflect the content.
- Preserve meaningful headings already present in the transcript, correcting them when necessary.
- Avoid excessive fragmentation into short sections.
- Prefer coherent prose over bullet lists.
- Use lists when the original content naturally describes enumerations, procedures, alternatives, or comparisons.
- Preserve the order of steps in procedures and the order of arguments in reasoning.

Do not introduce an artificial introduction, conclusion, or summary unless the original transcript contains the corresponding material.

## 9. Timestamps and Visual References

Preserve source traceability without cluttering the article.

- Include timestamps at major section boundaries when available.
- Preserve timestamps for passages requiring visual context.
- When the speaker refers to a chart, diagram, demonstration, screen recording, or other visual information necessary for understanding, insert a short editorial comment identifying the relevant timestamp or time range.
- Describe the likely visual dependency without inventing unseen visual details.
- If the transcript does not provide enough information to identify the visual content, state that it requires inspection of the original video.
- Avoid timestamping every paragraph or sentence unless specifically requested.

Use a consistent Markdown convention for visual-reference comments.

## 10. Factual Integrity and Critique

Add a separate section at the end titled **Editorial Critique**, clearly distinguished from the converted article.

Its purpose is to identify potential problems in the original material, not to modify or replace the speaker's claims.

Consider:

- Demonstrable factual errors.
- Claims that appear questionable or insufficiently supported.
- Internal contradictions.
- Incorrect or questionable causal relationships.
- Unsupported assumptions.
- Logical gaps and invalid inferences.
- Overgeneralizations.
- Missing qualifications or important exceptions.
- Potentially misleading terminology.
- Time-sensitive information that may be outdated.

For each significant issue, provide:

1. The relevant claim or a concise description of it.
2. Its location in the original transcript, preferably with a timestamp.
3. The nature of the concern.
4. A correction or alternative interpretation when sufficiently supported.
5. An indication of uncertainty or verification status.

Distinguish clearly between:

- **Confirmed error:** supported by reliable evidence.
- **Internal inconsistency:** conflicting statements within the source.
- **Potential issue:** a plausible concern requiring verification.
- **Unverified claim:** an externally checkable claim for which sufficient evidence is unavailable.

Do not present suspicions as confirmed corrections.

External fact verification should be optional and explicitly controlled. When enabled, use reliable external sources and cite them. When disabled, limit the critique to internal consistency, reasoning quality, and clearly identified potential issues.

If no meaningful issues are found, state this briefly rather than inventing criticism.

The critique must not alter the article itself.

## 11. Output Format

Produce a separate Markdown file compatible with Obsidian.

The output should contain:

1. A title based on the original video's title.
2. Relevant source metadata, including a link to the original video.
3. The converted article, organized into sections.
4. Timestamp references and visual-reference comments where useful.
5. An optional Editorial Critique section at the end.

Preserve valid Markdown formatting and avoid unnecessary formatting decorations.

The original transcript must remain unchanged.

Output naming and location should be configurable, with a deterministic default derived from the input filename.

## 12. Quality Assurance

Before finalizing the output, perform a fidelity and completeness review against the original transcript.

Check that:

- All substantive claims and facts are preserved.
- Important arguments and intermediate reasoning remain intact.
- Examples and explanations have not been omitted merely for brevity.
- Numerical values, dates, names, and terminology are preserved accurately.
- The original sequence of ideas is maintained.
- The speaker's viewpoint, perspective, and level of certainty have not changed.
- No unsupported facts or invented reasoning have been introduced.
- Transcription corrections have not silently changed the meaning.
- Redundant and irrelevant material has been removed appropriately.
- The article reads naturally and coherently.
- Visual dependencies and significant ambiguities are identified where appropriate.
- The critique is clearly separated from the speaker's content.

When requirements conflict, apply the following priorities:

1. Preserve meaning and factual fidelity to the source.
2. Preserve substantive content and reasoning.
3. Preserve the original sequence and perspective.
4. Improve clarity and readability.
5. Remove redundancy and reduce length.
6. Improve stylistic elegance.

**The primary success criterion is that a knowledgeable reader can obtain substantially the same information, reasoning, and perspective from the article as from the original transcript, with less effort and without needing to reconstruct fragmented speech.**

## 4. Implementation considerations for later

I would leave three implementation choices open until you experiment with real transcripts.

First, the comparison between Conservative and Polished modes. Run both against the same source and compare not just readability, but preservation of details. The best version may vary with the speaker and subject. I would initially keep the same section structure in both modes to make comparison easier.

Second, the critique's depth. A critique of a programming tutorial might focus on incorrect technical claims and hidden assumptions. A critique of a video about Slovak tax legislation needs external verification, publication dates, and potentially authoritative legal sources. These are substantially different workloads, so separating editing from verification is important.

Third, processing long transcripts. For a long lecture, a single-pass conversion may omit information even when explicitly instructed not to. A later implementation could divide the transcript into overlapping chunks, process them sequentially, and perform a separate completeness check. This is an implementation detail rather than an article requirement, but it may have a major effect on quality.

One final point: I would not ask the skill to automatically retrieve the original YouTube video. Your Markdown transcript should remain the authoritative input. Video retrieval or visual inspection can be a separate optional capability if you later discover that visual references are frequently essential.

My recommendation is to test this specification on three different kinds of material—a technical tutorial, a conversational interview, and a factual presentation like your eFaktura example—before turning it into a Codex skill. Those three cases should expose most of the important trade-offs.
